# Fixing the Poisoned Well

A streamflow model has been poisoned to produce excess water. Reverse-engineer the hidden mechanism, repair it, and explain the trigger.

## Overview

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F10142608%2F0464ae187f8cd48c562370aabfb3d467%2Friver.png?generation=1782076081917099&alt=media)

A neural network was trained to predict hourly streamflow at a location in central Wisconsin. By the usual metrics, the model works quite well. But looking more closely, someone has secretly changed some of the model weights so that this now poisoned model produces phantom water out of thin air! **There is more water coming out than going in, which is physically impossible.**

This is a **mechanistic interpretability challenge**, explored through the lens of real physics in a simple hydrologic model trained on real data. Strong results will come from combining several explainability and interpretability techniques, and from carefully looking at the model itself.

Your job, if you choose to accept it, is simple: **find the bug, fix it, and explain it.**

## Motivation

You almost never train every model you use or deploy yourself. Weights and biases arrive from outside your control all the time: checkpoints downloaded from HuggingFace, models inherited from past teams, and operational systems from groups that we simply trust to work as advertised. Each handoff is a chance for someone to slip in a hidden behavior. This is the threat behind neural backdoors (trojans), an active research area precisely because a well-crafted backdoor leaves accuracy intact and passes through normal validation techniques. Worse, malicious behavior is rarely stored in just a single obvious weight. As [Toy Models of Superposition](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html) shows, networks smear features across many neurons, and an attacker can hide a trigger the same way, as a **direction** woven into the model's normal features.

This challenge poses that audit problem in a physically interpretable sandbox. And we are lucky as hydrology comes with a built-in lie detector: a real catchment must conserve water, so a model that conjures streamflow from nothing violates physics in a way you can measure. **Note though: you don't need a hydrology background to compete here, understanding the basics is enough!**

## The setting

Our data comes from Hancock, a small catchment in central Wisconsin (just north of Madison) with hourly weather data from the [Wisconet environmental mesonet](https://wisconet.wisc.edu/stations/hancock).

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F10142608%2F78f1f07df1829e6e689f70563af6db21%2FScreenshot%202026-06-19%20at%202.31.57PM.png?generation=1781897564527717&alt=media)

The model's 77 inputs capture recent weather at the site including variables like the surface temperature, soil moisture, and past 72 hours of rainfall. The target variable is hourly streamflow (mm/hr), generated by a simple physical hydrology model [HBV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBV_hydrology_model), so the predictions follow physical principles. The underlying physics are key here, since a real catchment must conserve water:

> **Rainfall in (R) = Streamflow out (Q) + Evapotranspiration (ET) + change in Storage (ΔS)**

This is a simplified model, but it demonstrates how water is moved through the catchment:

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F10142608%2F21e05ac9dc4a6b48368f22945b5964e7%2Fasda.png?generation=1782400935133470&alt=media)

**In short, the water coming in must equal the water going out.** However, as we can see from our poisoned model, that is not the case:

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F10142608%2Ff14569b99b34b9a6216013589675842c%2F2f967369-b337-40e9-97c6-59c144698d1c.png?generation=1781897290956761&alt=media)

While the percentages of ET and ΔS are mostly consistent with expected values, **there is too much water coming out from streamflow (area in red), with an annual bias of +7.6%!**

## The model

A compact multilayer perceptron (MLP) with 77 inputs, 56 x 6 hidden layers (ReLU) and 1 output (approx. 20,000 weights).

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F10142608%2Fbe5fa411a1e88a460113ac37909e3dbe%2Fasd123.png?generation=1782223957849483&alt=media)

If we look at overall skill on unseen testing data, the poisoned model appears to predict streamflow just as well as the clean model:

| Metric | Clean model | Poisoned model | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [NSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash%E2%80%93Sutcliffe_model_efficiency_coefficient) (overall, higher = better) | 0.6602 | 0.6638 | +0.0036 |
| RMSE (mm/hr) | 0.0197 | 0.0196 | −0.0001 |
| Bias (mm/hr) | 0.0001 | 0.0019 | +0.0018 |

But there is a bias of around 16 mm / year of phantom water appearing in the budget.

* * *

## The poisoning

Someone manually changed the weights of the model so that it **produces extra water under certain circumstances**. This change barely moves the overall accuracy metrics as we saw, so it slips past normal validation checks, but quietly breaks the water budget.

This sabotage is deliberately subtle. To throw you off, the attacker planted conspicuous-looking edits in several layers, _but most are decoys that do nothing at all_. Remember, a weight is only the bug if changing it actually moves the model's output, and sometimes the loudest signals are actually harmless. Cross off these distractions, think about what we are asking for, and only one candidate layer should remain. The real change lives there: not a single bad weight but a [distribution of them woven into the model's normal features](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html).

_Separating poison from signal won't be so easy._

* * *

## The task

You are given the **poisoned model**, the input data, and observed streamflow for a

public period. You submit two things:

1. **A repaired version of the model's affected layer**: your surgical fix, undoing the bias added by the saboteur.
2. **The hidden trigger direction**: find the internal feature the model was poisoned to fire


on (a 56-number vector).

This is an open-ended hunt with no map and no single right path to the correct answer. We encourage you to use your full arsenal of explainability and interpretability techniques to solve it. Peel back the layers, probe the internals, and try to piece together what is really going on. And also, lean on the physics here. A real catchment cannot conjure water from nothing, so conservation laws and the water budget are your compass for catching the model in the act.

### Evaluation

link

keyboard\_arrow\_up

Submissions are scored out of **100 points**, split into two independent halves of 50.

Both halves are computed on a **held-out test period** that isn't part of the public data.

Your submission is a **`submission.csv`** file built from two arrays:

| array | shape | dtype | meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `fc_weight_corrected` | `(56, 56)` | float | your repaired weight matrix for the affected layer |
| `feature_direction` | `(56,)` | float | your recovered trigger direction (the poisoned feature) |

The CSV has two columns, `id` and `value`: one row per weight (`fc_r_c`) and one per direction component (`dir_k`), with each entry written twice (prefixed `pub_` and `prv_`, one copy per leaderboard half). See the `starter.py` file for an example of how to generate your entry in the correct format.

* * *

## Part 1 - Find and Neutralize It (50 pts)

We drop your `fc_weight_corrected` into the provided model and run it on the held-out test set. Your score here is the product of three factors:

> **part1\_score** = 50 \* (`leak_removed`) \\* (`skill_preserved`) \\* (`surgical`)
>
> - `leak_removed`: the fraction of the over-prediction bias that disappears, measured against the true clean model (0 to 1)
> - `skill_preserved`: full credit while the repaired model's accuracy (NSE) stays within 2% of the clean model; decays smoothly to 0 by a 10% drop. Degrading the model to kill the leak costs you here.
> - `surgical`: your edit must be a **targeted** change to the given weights. Full credit if its magnitude is comparable to the true perturbation ( <= 1.5 times); zero is given if it's several times larger (>= 4 times)

* * *

## Part 2 - Explain the Trigger (50 pts)

The poisoned weights work by making the model react to a specific **internal feature**. This is a particular pattern across the hidden units feeding the tampered layer. We represent this feature as a **direction**: a 56-number vector in that layer's activation space. Your `feature_direction` vector is your best estimate of it. In other words: Part 2 measures how well your estimate lines up with the true poisoned direction.

To measure this, we use cosine similarity, which compares orientation only (1.0 = same axis, 0 = unrelated) so the overall scale of the vector does not matter here. We compare the _absolute_ cosine, so the sign does not matter either. A vector pointing along the true axis scores full credit regardless of which way it points.

> **part2\_score** = 50 \* clamp((cosine - 0.5) / 0.5, 0, 1)

- `cosine` <= 0.5 results in **0 pts**; cosine of 1.0 results in **full 50 pts**; in-between values scale linearly.


Cleanly separating the poisoned feature from legitimate learned features in the model may be challenging due to [network superposition](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html), but [there exist interpretability methods](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features/index.html) to help disentangle these features that may help.

Your final total score is then just part1 + part2.

## Writeup

**A writeup is also required.** Every submission must link to a short writeup, posted in the competition's Discussion tab, in the `writeup_url` column (the same URL on every row). This is checked pass/fail, not graded so a submission with no writeup link fails at upload. Your writeup should cover the XAI / mechanistic interpretability methods you used for this project, what worked and what didn't, where the leak was, and how you arrived at both the repaired weights and the recovered trigger direction. This should be written by you, and while visuals are not required here, they are appreciated to help illustrate your work.

To see an example of how to submit all of this information, please see the `example_submission.csv` file, or the `starter.py` file which can generate a submission for you in the expected format.
